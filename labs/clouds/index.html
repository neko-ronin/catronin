01 / The field

Anatomy of a cloud

A Tumbler cloud is an authored volume with a procedural interior. The world places the cloud. The shader gives it a silhouette, depth, and light you can fly through.

01 Shape the billows
Start with a wide, low ellipsoid and seven uneven lobes. Take the maximum of their signed profiles, rotate the arrangement with the cell seed, then soften the flat base and box boundary. Each cell has a center, three dimensions, and a seed. Tumbler places 16 of them in a common 980–1,600 m altitude band.
02 Erode the edges
Pack three octaves of smooth value noise and two scales of inverted Worley noise into a repeating 48³ RGBA8 texture. Value noise roughens the body. The coarse Worley channel cuts scallops and the fine channel erodes the edges. Wind moves the noise through the anchored shape.
03 March through it
Intersect the camera ray with the box and stop it at the opaque world’s depth. Clamp entry to zero for a camera inside the cloud. March at half width and height, offset the samples with stable per-pixel jitter, and stop early when transmission is nearly zero. Composite in linear color before tone mapping.
04 Give it light
Trace five expanding samples toward the sun through the coarser density. Cache direct and soft transmission in a padded 32³ lattice per cell, typically refreshed about twice per simulation second. Height-tinted ambient and a forward-scattering term give cool bases and bright backlit edges. The same field casts shadows into a separate 256² ground map.

Distant cloud color fades toward the same clear-air sky color as the background. The Markdown guide includes the density coefficients, cache layout, and depth-clipping pseudocode.

02 / The revisions

What changed

  1. From painted sky to real volume

    The original sky painted broad cloud cover in 2D. The first fly-through version placed nine volumes with three smooth lobes each. Later passes widened the coverage, shaped unequal turrets and flat bases, layered the erosion, and shared the sky’s haze. The sunlight cache kept the fine detail in the view march while amortizing the coarser sun-ray work.

  2. Steadier edges in flight

    The view march doubled from 48 to 96 samples. This reduced visible stepping at cloud edges during flight, with a measurable GPU cost. A separate optimization skips provably empty samples above the cloud layer and preserves the positions of all occupied samples.

Horizontal bands in the valley preview

Three.js r180 applied the canvas pixel ratio to offscreen scissor coordinates that were already in atlas texels. With r187 the cloud-lighting atlas addressed its texels directly. The bands disappeared, and the atlas matched byte for byte across five render scales from 0.7 to 2. The density and all 96 view samples stayed the same. Three.js migration notes

03 / The port

One cloud first

Build one cloud at a fixed seed, sun, and wind. Use your engine’s depth and color conventions. Start with the full density march and direct sun samples, then profile the camera path before deciding where to spend your sample budget.

  1. Record a fixed path from below, across the edge, through the interior, and out above. Include a ridge crossing in front of the cloud and try both low and high sun.
  2. Compare with a higher-sample render and report median and 95th-percentile GPU time. Check for stepping, seams, depth leaks, and lighting bands at different render scales.
  3. Add the sunlight cache, more cells, and ground shadows. Verify that projected shadows accumulate in either draw order. If cloud volumes frequently intersect, integrate overlapping density in the same ray march.

For a coding agent, hand over the Markdown, a reference capture, the target renderer’s depth and color conventions, and one fixed camera route. Ask for that fly-through and its timings as the first deliverable.
